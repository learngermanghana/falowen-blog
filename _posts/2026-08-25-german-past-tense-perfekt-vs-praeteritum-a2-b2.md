## German Perfekt or Präteritum? The Difference Learners Need to Know

German has more than one way to talk about the past, and this can confuse learners who expect one simple equivalent of the English past tense. Two forms matter especially from A2 onward: **Perfekt** and **Präteritum**.

The good news is that you do not need to choose randomly. In modern German, the choice often depends on **how you are communicating** and **which verb you are using**.

Compare:

> **Ich habe gestern lange gearbeitet.**  
> I worked for a long time yesterday.

> **Ich arbeitete gestern lange.**  
> I worked for a long time yesterday.

Both sentences describe a completed past event. However, the first sounds much more natural in ordinary conversation, while the second is more typical of written narration.

This guide shows A2–B2 learners how to make that choice confidently.

## 1. What Is the Perfekt?

The **Perfekt** normally consists of **haben or sein + Partizip II**.

> Ich **habe** Deutsch **gelernt**.  
> I learned / have learned German.

> Wir **sind** nach Berlin **gefahren**.  
> We travelled to Berlin.

> Sie **hat** ihre Hausaufgaben **gemacht**.  
> She did her homework.

In a main clause, the conjugated auxiliary verb occupies the normal verb position and the Partizip II usually goes to the end.

**Pattern:**

> Subject + haben/sein + ... + Partizip II

For everyday spoken German, Perfekt is the most important past tense for many verbs.

## 2. When Do You Use haben and When Do You Use sein?

Most verbs form the Perfekt with **haben**:

- lernen → **hat gelernt**
- machen → **hat gemacht**
- kaufen → **hat gekauft**
- sehen → **hat gesehen**
- arbeiten → **hat gearbeitet**

**sein** is common with verbs that express movement from one place to another or a change of state, as well as a few important verbs that simply have to be learned with sein:

- gehen → **ist gegangen**
- kommen → **ist gekommen**
- fahren → **ist gefahren**
- aufstehen → **ist aufgestanden**
- werden → **ist geworden**
- bleiben → **ist geblieben**

Examples:

> **Wir sind früh aufgestanden.**  
> We got up early.

> **Danach sind wir zum Bahnhof gefahren.**  
> Afterwards we went to the station.

A useful study habit is to learn an irregular verb together with its complete past-tense information: **fahren – fuhr – ist gefahren**, not simply *fahren = to drive/go*.

## 3. What Is the Präteritum?

The **Präteritum** is a one-word past-tense form.

> Ich **lernte** jeden Abend.  
> I studied every evening.

> Er **ging** nach Hause.  
> He went home.

> Wir **sahen** einen interessanten Film.  
> We saw an interesting film.

Regular verbs commonly use **-te** endings:

| Person | lernen |
|---|---|
| ich | lernte |
| du | lerntest |
| er/sie/es | lernte |
| wir | lernten |
| ihr | lerntet |
| sie/Sie | lernten |

Irregular verbs often change their stem and therefore need to be memorised:

| Infinitive | Präteritum | Perfekt |
|---|---|---|
| gehen | ging | ist gegangen |
| kommen | kam | ist gekommen |
| sehen | sah | hat gesehen |
| finden | fand | hat gefunden |
| schreiben | schrieb | hat geschrieben |
| sprechen | sprach | hat gesprochen |

## 4. Speaking: Perfekt Is Usually Your Safe Choice

When talking about completed events in everyday conversation, learners can normally rely on Perfekt.

If someone asks:

> **Was hast du am Wochenende gemacht?**  
> What did you do at the weekend?

A natural answer is:

> **Ich habe meine Freunde besucht. Danach sind wir essen gegangen. Am Abend haben wir einen Film gesehen.**  
> I visited my friends. Afterwards we went out to eat. In the evening we watched a film.

This is especially useful for A2 speaking tasks about holidays, weekends, experiences and daily life.

## 5. But sein, haben and Modal Verbs Often Use Präteritum in Speech

There is an important exception to the simple “speaking = Perfekt” rule. **sein**, **haben** and modal verbs are very commonly heard in the Präteritum even in conversation.

Instead of:

> Ich **bin müde gewesen**.

German speakers normally prefer:

> Ich **war müde**.  
> I was tired.

Instead of a heavy Perfekt construction, you will often hear:

> **Ich hatte keine Zeit.**  
> I didn't have time.

> **Ich musste arbeiten.**  
> I had to work.

> **Wir konnten nicht kommen.**  
> We couldn't come.

> **Sie wollte zu Hause bleiben.**  
> She wanted to stay at home.

These forms are worth learning early:

| Present | Präteritum | English |
|---|---|---|
| ist | war | was |
| hat | hatte | had |
| kann | konnte | could / was able to |
| muss | musste | had to |
| will | wollte | wanted to |
| darf | durfte | was allowed to |
| soll | sollte | was supposed to |

## 6. Writing: Präteritum Becomes More Important

Präteritum is especially common in written narratives, stories, biographies, reports and literary texts.

> **Der Mann öffnete die Tür, sah sich um und ging langsam ins Zimmer.**  
> The man opened the door, looked around and slowly entered the room.

Using Perfekt for every verb here would make the narration heavier:

> Der Mann hat die Tür geöffnet, hat sich umgesehen und ist langsam ins Zimmer gegangen.

That version is grammatically possible, but it sounds more like someone recounting events in conversation than a written narrative.

At B1 and B2, learners should therefore be able to **understand Präteritum easily**, even if they still use Perfekt frequently in their own speech.

## 7. Perfekt and Präteritum Usually Do Not Mean “Recent” vs “Distant” Past

English speakers sometimes assume that Perfekt must describe recent events and Präteritum older events. That is not a reliable German rule.

You can say:

> **Ich habe vor zehn Jahren in Berlin gewohnt.**  
> I lived in Berlin ten years ago.

The event happened a long time ago, but Perfekt is still possible in conversation.

Similarly:

> **Gestern war ich krank.**  
> I was ill yesterday.

The event is recent, but Präteritum with **sein** is completely natural.

Think about **register and verb choice**, not simply distance in time.

## 8. Word Order in Subordinate Clauses

Perfekt becomes particularly interesting when you use connectors such as **weil**, **dass** or **obwohl**.

Main clause:

> **Ich habe viel gelernt.**

Subordinate clause:

> **..., weil ich viel gelernt habe.**  
> ..., because I studied a lot.

With **sein**:

> **..., weil wir spät angekommen sind.**  
> ..., because we arrived late.

The auxiliary moves to the end of the subordinate clause.

Präteritum remains a single conjugated verb:

> **..., weil ich keine Zeit hatte.**  
> ..., because I had no time.

This distinction becomes increasingly useful when writing longer B1–B2 sentences.

## 9. Common Mistakes to Avoid

### Mistake 1: Using haben with every verb

Incorrect:

> Ich **habe** nach Hause gegangen.

Correct:

> Ich **bin** nach Hause gegangen.

### Mistake 2: Forgetting the Partizip II at the end

Incorrect:

> Ich habe gestern einen Film **sehen**.

Correct:

> Ich habe gestern einen Film **gesehen**.

### Mistake 3: Treating every movement verb as automatically sein

The rule is more nuanced than “movement = sein.” For example:

> Ich **habe** das Auto gefahren.

Here *fahren* is used transitively: you drove the car. Learn verbs in real sentence patterns rather than relying only on shortcuts.

### Mistake 4: Avoiding Präteritum completely

Even if you prefer Perfekt in conversation, you need forms such as **war, hatte, konnte, musste, wollte** very early. At higher levels, strong Präteritum forms are also essential for reading.

## 10. A Simple A2–B2 Learning Strategy

At **A2**, focus on building correct Perfekt sentences and learning whether common verbs use *haben* or *sein*. Memorise *war* and *hatte* as high-frequency Präteritum forms.

At **B1**, add the Präteritum of modal verbs and frequent irregular verbs. Practise switching between spoken-style Perfekt and written-style Präteritum.

At **B2**, pay attention to register. Read news, narratives and formal texts and notice why authors choose one past tense over another. Practise using past forms naturally inside subordinate clauses and longer arguments.

## Mini Practice

Change these sentences into the past. Choose a natural form for the context.

1. **Ich bin müde.** → Yesterday: ________
2. **Wir fahren nach Hamburg.** → Spoken account: ________
3. **Er muss lange arbeiten.** → Yesterday: ________
4. **Sie sieht den Mann und geht weg.** → Written story: ________
5. **Ich lerne viel, weil ich eine Prüfung habe.** → Past: ________

Possible answers:

1. **Gestern war ich müde.**
2. **Wir sind nach Hamburg gefahren.**
3. **Er musste gestern lange arbeiten.**
4. **Sie sah den Mann und ging weg.**
5. **Ich habe viel gelernt, weil ich eine Prüfung hatte.**

## Final Learner Tip

Do not try to memorise two enormous isolated tense tables. Learn past forms through **useful verb packages**:

> gehen – ging – ist gegangen  
> sehen – sah – hat gesehen  
> sein – war – ist gewesen  
> haben – hatte – hat gehabt

Then practise each verb in a real sentence. This gives you the grammar, meaning and natural context at the same time.
