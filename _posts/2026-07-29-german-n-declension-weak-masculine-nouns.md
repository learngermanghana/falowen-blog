## Why German N-Declension Matters

Many German learners know that articles change with the case:

> **der Student** → **den Studenten**

But the second change is easy to miss. The article changes from **der** to **den**, and the noun itself also receives **-en**.

This pattern is called **N-Deklination**, **weak noun declension**, or **the declension of weak masculine nouns**. It mainly affects a special group of masculine nouns.

You need this grammar when you talk about people, professions, animals and everyday situations:

> Ich spreche mit **dem Kollegen**.  
> I am speaking with the colleague.

> Wir besuchen **den Patienten**.  
> We are visiting the patient.

> Das Fahrrad gehört **dem Jungen**.  
> The bicycle belongs to the boy.

The rule is systematic once you know which nouns belong to the group.

## The Core Rule

A weak masculine noun usually has its normal form only in the **nominative singular**.

In the **accusative, dative and genitive singular**, it normally adds **-n** or **-en**.

Take the noun **der Student**:

| Case | Question | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | Wer? | der Student | **Der Student** lernt Deutsch. |
| Accusative | Wen? | den Studenten | Ich sehe **den Studenten**. |
| Dative | Wem? | dem Studenten | Ich helfe **dem Studenten**. |
| Genitive | Wessen? | des Studenten | Das Buch **des Studenten** ist neu. |

The key idea is:

**Only the nominative singular usually stays unchanged.**

All other singular cases take **-n** or **-en**.

## A Simple Memory Formula

Use this short formula:

**Nominative singular: no extra ending**  
**Accusative, dative and genitive singular: -n or -en**

For **der Kollege**:

> **Der Kollege** arbeitet heute.  
> The colleague is working today.

> Ich rufe **den Kollegen** an.  
> I am calling the colleague.

> Ich spreche mit **dem Kollegen**.  
> I am speaking with the colleague.

> Das Büro **des Kollegen** ist oben.  
> The colleague’s office is upstairs.

## Group 1: Masculine Nouns Ending in -e

Many masculine nouns referring to people or animals end in **-e**. They usually add only **-n**, because the noun already ends in **e**.

Common examples include:

| Nominative | Other singular cases | English |
|---|---|---|
| der Junge | den Jungen | boy |
| der Kollege | den Kollegen | colleague |
| der Kunde | den Kunden | customer |
| der Experte | den Experten | expert |
| der Zeuge | den Zeugen | witness |
| der Löwe | den Löwen | lion |
| der Hase | den Hasen | hare, rabbit |

Examples:

> Der Verkäufer spricht mit **dem Kunden**.  
> The salesperson is speaking with the customer.

> Die Polizei befragt **den Zeugen**.  
> The police are questioning the witness.

> Das Verhalten **des Löwen** ist ungewöhnlich.  
> The lion’s behaviour is unusual.

Notice that the added ending is **-n**, not **-en**, because these nouns already end in **-e**.

## Group 2: International Masculine Nouns

Many international words referring to people are weak masculine nouns. They often have endings such as:

- **-ant**
- **-ent**
- **-ist**
- **-oge**
- **-at**
- **-nom**

Common examples:

| Nominative | Accusative / dative / genitive | English |
|---|---|---|
| der Student | den Studenten | student |
| der Praktikant | den Praktikanten | intern |
| der Präsident | den Präsidenten | president |
| der Journalist | den Journalisten | journalist |
| der Polizist | den Polizisten | police officer |
| der Biologe | den Biologen | biologist |
| der Diplomat | den Diplomaten | diplomat |
| der Ökonom | den Ökonomen | economist |

Examples:

> Die Professorin erklärt **dem Studenten** die Aufgabe.  
> The professor explains the task to the student.

> Der Artikel stammt von **einem Journalisten**.  
> The article was written by a journalist.

> Die Regierung empfängt **den Diplomaten**.  
> The government receives the diplomat.

These nouns usually add **-en** in the non-nominative singular forms.

## Group 3: Important Nouns You Must Learn Individually

Some common weak masculine nouns do not belong to one obvious ending group. They are worth learning as complete pairs.

| Nominative | Other singular cases | English |
|---|---|---|
| der Mensch | den Menschen | person, human being |
| der Herr | den Herrn | gentleman, Mr. |
| der Nachbar | den Nachbarn | neighbour |
| der Bauer | den Bauern | farmer |
| der Fürst | den Fürsten | prince |
| der Held | den Helden | hero |
| der Bär | den Bären | bear |
| der Elefant | den Elefanten | elephant |

Examples:

> Jeder **Mensch** braucht soziale Kontakte.  
> Every person needs social contact.

> Wir helfen **dem Menschen** dort.  
> We are helping the person over there.

> Ich habe gestern **den Nachbarn** gesehen.  
> I saw the neighbour yesterday.

> Die Geschichte handelt von **einem Helden**.  
> The story is about a hero.

## The Special Case: der Herr

The noun **der Herr** is especially common in formal communication.

Its forms are:

| Case | Form |
|---|---|
| Nominative | der Herr |
| Accusative | den Herrn |
| Dative | dem Herrn |
| Genitive | des Herrn |

Examples:

> **Herr Müller** wartet draußen.  
> Mr Müller is waiting outside.

> Ich möchte mit **Herrn Müller** sprechen.  
> I would like to speak with Mr Müller.

> Die E-Mail kommt von **Herrn Müller**.  
> The email comes from Mr Müller.

A frequent learner mistake is:

> Ich spreche mit Herr Müller.

In standard German, the better form is:

> Ich spreche mit **Herrn Müller**.

## How N-Declension Works with Articles and Adjectives

The noun ending is independent of the article and adjective endings. All parts must match the case.

### Accusative

> Ich treffe **den neuen Kollegen**.  
> I am meeting the new colleague.

Here we have:

- **den** = accusative masculine article
- **neuen** = adjective ending
- **Kollegen** = N-declension ending

### Dative

> Ich arbeite mit **einem erfahrenen Experten**.  
> I work with an experienced expert.

Here we have:

- **einem** = dative masculine article
- **erfahrenen** = adjective ending
- **Experten** = N-declension ending

### Genitive

> Die Meinung **des bekannten Journalisten** war interessant.  
> The well-known journalist’s opinion was interesting.

The article, adjective and noun all show grammatical information.

## N-Declension in the Plural

The plural often looks similar to the weak singular forms:

> der Student → die Studenten  
> der Kollege → die Kollegen  
> der Journalist → die Journalisten

Compare:

> Ich sehe **den Studenten**.  
> I see the student.

> Ich sehe **die Studenten**.  
> I see the students.

The noun form is the same, but the article tells you whether it is singular or plural.

This is another reason to pay attention to the complete noun phrase, not only the noun ending.

## Important Exception: der Name

A small group of masculine nouns follows a related pattern but also adds **-s** in the genitive.

The most important example is **der Name**:

| Case | Form |
|---|---|
| Nominative | der Name |
| Accusative | den Namen |
| Dative | dem Namen |
| Genitive | des Namens |

Examples:

> Ich kenne **den Namen** nicht.  
> I do not know the name.

> Die Bedeutung **des Namens** ist interessant.  
> The meaning of the name is interesting.

Other nouns with a similar mixed pattern include **der Gedanke**, **der Glaube**, **der Wille** and **der Buchstabe**.

For example:

> der Gedanke → den Gedanken → des Gedankens

These are sometimes called **mixed masculine nouns** because they combine weak endings with a genitive **-s**.

## Common Mistakes with Weak Masculine Nouns

### Mistake 1: Changing only the article

Incorrect:

> Ich sehe den Student.

Correct:

> Ich sehe **den Studenten**.

### Mistake 2: Forgetting the ending after a preposition

Incorrect:

> Ich spreche mit dem Kollege.

Correct:

> Ich spreche mit **dem Kollegen**.

### Mistake 3: Adding the ending in the nominative

Incorrect:

> Der Studenten kommt aus Ghana.

Correct:

> **Der Student** kommt aus Ghana.

### Mistake 4: Treating every masculine noun as weak

Not every masculine noun takes N-declension.

For example:

> der Lehrer → den Lehrer  
> der Fahrer → dem Fahrer  
> der Computer → des Computers

These nouns do not add **-n** or **-en** in the accusative or dative singular.

## How to Recognize an N-Declension Noun

Use this three-step check.

### Step 1: Is the noun masculine?

N-declension mainly affects masculine nouns.

### Step 2: Does it refer to a person or animal?

Many weak nouns describe people or animals:

- der Student
- der Kunde
- der Junge
- der Löwe

### Step 3: Does it have a typical ending?

Pay special attention to masculine nouns ending in:

- **-e**
- **-ant**
- **-ent**
- **-ist**
- **-oge**
- **-at**
- **-nom**

This is a strong clue, but not an absolute rule. Learn important nouns together with their accusative form:

> der Student – den Studenten  
> der Kollege – den Kollegen  
> der Mensch – den Menschen

## A Better Vocabulary-Learning Method

Do not learn only:

> Student = student

Learn the noun as a mini-pattern:

> **der Student – den Studenten – mit dem Studenten**

This gives you:

- the gender
- the accusative form
- the dative form
- the N-declension ending

You can also create one personal sentence:

> Ich lerne mit einem Studenten aus Berlin.

Personal examples are usually easier to remember than isolated grammar tables.

## N-Declension in B1 and B2 Writing

Weak masculine nouns appear frequently in formal and academic topics.

### Work and education

> Die Firma sucht **einen qualifizierten Praktikanten**.

> Der Kurs hilft **jedem Teilnehmer** beim Prüfungstraining.

### Health

> Die Ärztin untersucht **den Patienten**.

> Die Daten **des Patienten** müssen geschützt werden.

### Society and media

> Die Meinung **des Journalisten** wurde öffentlich diskutiert.

> Der Staat sollte **jedem Menschen** gleiche Chancen bieten.

Using these forms correctly makes your writing more precise and natural.

## Quick Practice

Complete each sentence with the correct form of the noun.

1. Der Professor spricht mit dem ________. (**Student**)
2. Ich besuche meinen neuen ________. (**Kollege**)
3. Die Polizei sucht den ________. (**Zeuge**)
4. Das ist das Büro des ________. (**Präsident**)
5. Wir helfen einem jungen ________. (**Mensch**)
6. Ich kenne den ________ der Firma nicht. (**Name**)
7. Die Reportage stammt von einem bekannten ________. (**Journalist**)
8. ________ Weber ist heute nicht im Büro. (**Herr**)

### Answers

1. dem **Studenten**
2. meinen neuen **Kollegen**
3. den **Zeugen**
4. des **Präsidenten**
5. einem jungen **Menschen**
6. den **Namen**
7. einem bekannten **Journalisten**
8. **Herr** Weber

Notice the contrast in number 8: **Herr Weber** is nominative, so it does not change. In a sentence such as *Ich spreche mit Herrn Weber*, it becomes **Herrn**.

## A Five-Minute Practice Routine

Choose three weak masculine nouns and practise them in four cases.

For example, with **der Kunde**:

> **Der Kunde** wartet.  
> Ich begrüße **den Kunden**.  
> Ich helfe **dem Kunden**.  
> Die Bestellung **des Kunden** ist fertig.

Then change the noun:

- der Student
- der Kollege
- der Patient
- der Journalist

This short exercise trains case recognition and noun endings at the same time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is N-declension in German?

N-declension is a pattern used by certain masculine nouns. They normally add **-n** or **-en** in the accusative, dative and genitive singular, while the nominative singular remains unchanged.

### Why is it “den Studenten” and not “den Student”?

**Student** is a weak masculine noun. In the accusative singular, it takes the ending **-en**: **den Studenten**.

### Do all masculine nouns use N-declension?

No. Nouns such as **der Lehrer**, **der Fahrer** and **der Computer** do not follow this pattern. You must recognize the common weak-noun groups and learn frequent exceptions.

### Is “Herr” an N-declension noun?

Yes. Outside the nominative singular, **der Herr** becomes **den Herrn**, **dem Herrn** and **des Herrn**.

### What is the difference between “der Name” and normal weak nouns?

**Der Name** takes **-n** in the accusative and dative but **-ns** in the genitive: **den Namen**, **dem Namen**, **des Namens**. It belongs to a related mixed-declension group.
