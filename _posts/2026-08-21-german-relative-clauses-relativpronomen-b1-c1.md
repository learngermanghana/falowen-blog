## What Are Relative Clauses in German?

German **relative clauses (Relativsätze)** let you add information about a person, object or idea without starting a completely new sentence. They are especially useful from B1 onward because they help your German sound more connected and precise.

Compare:

> Das ist die Frau. Sie arbeitet in Berlin.  
> That is the woman. She works in Berlin.

A more natural version is:

> Das ist die Frau, **die in Berlin arbeitet**.  
> That is the woman **who works in Berlin**.

The second sentence uses a relative clause. Notice two important features: there is a comma before the clause, and the conjugated verb **arbeitet** goes to the end.

## The Basic Rule: Choose the Pronoun by Gender and Case

A relative pronoun agrees with the noun it refers to in **gender and number**, but its **case depends on its job inside the relative clause**.

This is the key to understanding German relative pronouns.

| Case | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | der | die | das | die |
| Accusative | den | die | das | die |
| Dative | dem | der | dem | denen |
| Genitive | dessen | deren | dessen | deren |

Do not choose the case simply because the noun had that case in the main sentence. Ask what the relative pronoun does **inside its own clause**.

## Nominative Relative Pronouns

Use the nominative when the relative pronoun is the subject of the relative clause.

> Der Mann, **der dort wartet**, ist mein Lehrer.  
> The man **who is waiting there** is my teacher.

Here, **der** performs the action *wartet*, so it is nominative.

> Ich kenne eine Studentin, **die sehr schnell lernt**.  
> I know a student **who learns very quickly**.

> Das Buch, **das auf dem Tisch liegt**, gehört mir.  
> The book **that is lying on the table** belongs to me.

## Accusative Relative Pronouns

Use the accusative when the relative pronoun is the direct object.

> Der Mann, **den ich gestern gesehen habe**, ist Arzt.  
> The man **whom I saw yesterday** is a doctor.

Why **den**? The noun *Mann* is masculine, and inside the relative clause we say:

> Ich habe **den Mann** gesehen.

So the relative pronoun must also be accusative masculine: **den**.

Another example:

> Die Prüfung, **die wir nächste Woche schreiben**, ist wichtig.  
> The exam **that we are taking next week** is important.

## Dative Relative Pronouns

Use the dative when the verb or structure requires a dative object.

> Der Student, **dem ich geholfen habe**, hat bestanden.  
> The student **whom I helped** passed.

The verb **helfen** requires dative: *Ich helfe dem Studenten.* Therefore the relative pronoun is **dem**.

> Die Kollegin, **der ich vertraue**, arbeitet heute von zu Hause.  
> The colleague **whom I trust** is working from home today.

This is why learning verbs together with their cases is so useful.

## Relative Clauses with Prepositions

When a preposition belongs to the relative clause, place it **before the relative pronoun**.

> Das ist die Firma, **für die ich arbeite**.  
> That is the company **I work for**.

> Der Kurs, **an dem ich teilnehme**, dauert drei Monate.  
> The course **I am taking part in** lasts three months.

> Die Person, **mit der ich gesprochen habe**, war sehr freundlich.  
> The person **I spoke with** was very friendly.

The preposition determines the case. For example, **mit** always takes dative, so *die Person* becomes **mit der**.

This structure is extremely useful in B2 and C1 writing because it allows you to combine ideas elegantly.

## Word Order: Put the Verb at the End

A German relative clause is a subordinate clause, so the conjugated verb normally goes to the end.

Incorrect:

> Das ist der Lehrer, der **unterrichtet Deutsch**.

Correct:

> Das ist der Lehrer, der **Deutsch unterrichtet**.

With a perfect tense, the verbal elements collect at the end:

> Das ist der Film, **den wir gestern gesehen haben**.  
> That is the film **we watched yesterday**.

With a modal verb:

> Das ist eine Aufgabe, **die wir heute erledigen müssen**.  
> That is a task **that we have to complete today**.

A useful editing habit is to find every relative clause in your writing and check its final verb before submitting your work.

## Genitive Relative Pronouns: dessen and deren

At higher levels, **dessen** and **deren** are useful for expressing possession.

> Der Mann, **dessen Auto gestohlen wurde**, hat die Polizei angerufen.  
> The man **whose car was stolen** called the police.

> Die Frau, **deren Tochter in Deutschland studiert**, spricht sehr gut Deutsch.  
> The woman **whose daughter studies in Germany** speaks German very well.

Use **dessen** after a masculine or neuter antecedent and **deren** after a feminine or plural antecedent.

These forms are particularly valuable in formal B2–C1 texts because they can make sentences more concise.

## A B1 Sentence Upgraded toward C1

B1-style version:

> Viele Menschen arbeiten im Homeoffice. Diese Menschen sparen Zeit.

Connected version:

> Viele Menschen, **die im Homeoffice arbeiten**, sparen Zeit.

More advanced version:

> Beschäftigte, **deren Tätigkeiten ortsunabhängig ausgeübt werden können**, profitieren häufig von flexibleren Arbeitsmodellen.

You do not need to make every sentence complicated. The goal is to use relative clauses when they make relationships between ideas clearer.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Copying the case from the main clause

Wrong:

> Ich spreche mit dem Mann, **dem** du gestern gesehen hast.

Correct:

> Ich spreche mit dem Mann, **den** du gestern gesehen hast.

Although *dem Mann* is dative in the main clause because of **mit**, the relative pronoun is the direct object of **gesehen hast**, so it must be accusative: **den**.

### 2. Forgetting verb-final word order

Wrong:

> Die Frau, die arbeitet hier, kommt aus Hamburg.

Correct:

> Die Frau, die **hier arbeitet**, kommt aus Hamburg.

### 3. Forgetting the comma

Relative clauses are separated from the main clause with commas.

> Mein Bruder, **der in München wohnt**, besucht uns morgen.

### 4. Using English-style preposition placement

Avoid copying structures such as *the person who I spoke with* word for word.

German normally puts the preposition before the pronoun:

> die Person, **mit der** ich gesprochen habe

## Practical Tips for Learning Relative Clauses

First, practise only **der, die and das** as nominative relative pronouns. Then add accusative **den**, followed by dative **dem, der, denen**. Once those forms feel natural, practise preposition combinations such as **mit dem**, **für die**, **an der** and **über das**.

When you learn a new verb, note whether it needs accusative, dative or a fixed preposition. That knowledge makes choosing a relative pronoun much easier.

For writing practice, take two short sentences and combine them:

> Ich besuche einen Kurs. Der Kurs beginnt am Montag.

becomes:

> Ich besuche einen Kurs, **der am Montag beginnt**.

Doing five transformations like this each day is more useful than memorising the pronoun table without context.

## Quick Practice

Choose the correct relative pronoun.

1. Das ist der Lehrer, ___ uns Deutsch unterrichtet.
2. Kennst du den Mann, ___ ich gestern getroffen habe?
3. Die Studentin, ___ ich geholfen habe, hat die Prüfung bestanden.
4. Das ist das Thema, über ___ wir gesprochen haben.
5. Die Frau, ___ Sohn in Wien studiert, kommt aus Ghana.

**Answers:** 1. der, 2. den, 3. der, 4. das, 5. deren.

Now create three of your own relative clauses: one nominative, one accusative and one with a preposition.

## Why Relative Clauses Matter for German Exams

At B1, relative clauses help you connect simple ideas instead of writing a series of short sentences. At B2, you should be comfortable changing case and using prepositions. At C1, structures such as **dessen/deren** and carefully chosen relative clauses can help you express complex relationships precisely.

Accuracy still matters more than complexity. A correct, clear relative clause is better than an unnecessarily complicated sentence with several case and word-order errors.
