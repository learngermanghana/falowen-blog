## German Relative Clauses Without the Confusion

Relative clauses are one of the most useful tools for making your German sound more natural and precise. Instead of producing several short sentences, you can connect information about a person, object or idea in one sentence.

Compare:

**Das ist der Mann. Der Mann arbeitet in Berlin.**  
That is the man. The man works in Berlin.

A German speaker would normally combine these ideas:

**Das ist der Mann, der in Berlin arbeitet.**  
That is the man who works in Berlin.

The second part — **der in Berlin arbeitet** — is a relative clause (*Relativsatz*).

The system becomes much easier once you understand two questions: **Which noun does the relative pronoun refer to?** and **What job does the pronoun perform inside the relative clause?**

## What Is a Relative Clause in German?

A relative clause gives additional information about a noun in the main clause.

**Ich kenne eine Frau, die fünf Sprachen spricht.**  
I know a woman who speaks five languages.

The relative clause **die fünf Sprachen spricht** tells us more about *eine Frau*.

German relative clauses are separated from the main clause by a **comma**, and the conjugated verb normally goes to the **end** of the relative clause.

**Das ist das Buch, das ich gestern gekauft habe.**  
That is the book that I bought yesterday.

Notice that **habe** comes at the end.

## The Most Important Rule: Gender Comes From the Noun, Case Comes From the Clause

This rule solves many relative-pronoun problems.

The **gender and number** of the relative pronoun come from the noun it refers to. However, its **case** depends on its grammatical function inside the relative clause.

Consider:

**Der Mann, der dort steht, ist mein Lehrer.**  
The man who is standing there is my teacher.

*Mann* is masculine. Inside the relative clause, the man is the subject of *steht*. Therefore we need masculine nominative: **der**.

Now compare:

**Der Mann, den ich sehe, ist mein Lehrer.**  
The man whom I see is my teacher.

The noun is still masculine, but inside the relative clause *ich* is the subject and the man is the direct object. Therefore we need masculine accusative: **den**.

## German Relative Pronouns: Quick Table

| Case | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | der | die | das | die |
| Accusative | den | die | das | die |
| Dative | dem | der | dem | denen |
| Genitive | dessen | deren | dessen | deren |

For most B1 learners, mastering nominative, accusative and dative first gives the biggest benefit.

## Nominative Relative Pronouns

Use nominative when the relative pronoun is the **subject** of the relative clause.

**Ich habe einen Freund, der in München wohnt.**  
I have a friend who lives in Munich.

Who lives in Munich? **Der Freund.** Therefore *der* is nominative.

**Das ist eine App, die beim Deutschlernen hilft.**  
That is an app that helps with learning German.

**Wir suchen ein Hotel, das nicht zu teuer ist.**  
We are looking for a hotel that is not too expensive.

**Die Studenten, die heute fehlen, schreiben den Test morgen.**  
The students who are absent today will take the test tomorrow.

## Accusative Relative Pronouns

Use accusative when the relative pronoun is the **direct object** inside the relative clause.

**Das ist der Kollege, den ich gestern getroffen habe.**  
That is the colleague whom I met yesterday.

Ask: *Wen habe ich getroffen?* — **den Kollegen**.

**Die Wohnung, die wir besichtigt haben, ist sehr modern.**  
The apartment that we viewed is very modern.

**Das Handy, das ich kaufen möchte, ist zu teuer.**  
The phone that I want to buy is too expensive.

A common mistake is choosing the case from the main sentence. Always determine the pronoun's role **inside the relative clause**.

## Dative Relative Pronouns

Use dative when the verb or construction inside the relative clause requires dative.

**Der Student, dem ich geholfen habe, kommt aus Ghana.**  
The student whom I helped comes from Ghana.

The verb **helfen** takes dative: *jemandem helfen*. Therefore masculine *der Student* becomes **dem**.

**Die Frau, der ich geschrieben habe, arbeitet an der Universität.**  
The woman whom I wrote to works at the university.

**Die Leute, denen wir danken möchten, sind schon gegangen.**  
The people whom we would like to thank have already left.

Remember the plural dative relative pronoun: **denen**.

## Relative Clauses with Prepositions

A preposition comes **before** the relative pronoun. The preposition determines the case.

**Das ist die Firma, bei der ich arbeite.**  
That is the company where I work.

**Der Freund, mit dem ich Deutsch lerne, wohnt in Accra.**  
The friend with whom I learn German lives in Accra.

**Das Thema, über das wir sprechen, ist wichtig.**  
The topic that we are talking about is important.

Do not remove the preposition simply because English sometimes allows it at the end. German normally keeps the preposition directly before the relative pronoun.

## What Happens to Word Order?

A relative clause behaves like a subordinate clause. The conjugated verb goes to the end.

**Ich kenne den Mann, der dort arbeitet.**

With a modal verb:

**Das ist die Prüfung, die ich nächste Woche schreiben muss.**  
That is the exam that I have to take next week.

With Perfekt:

**Das ist der Film, den wir gestern gesehen haben.**  
That is the film that we watched yesterday.

This verb-final pattern is worth practising until it becomes automatic.

## Common Mistakes German Learners Make

### 1. Automatically repeating the article

Incorrect: **Der Mann, der ich sehe, ...**  
Correct: **Der Mann, den ich sehe, ...**

The noun is masculine, but the pronoun is the accusative object of *sehen*.

### 2. Putting the verb in second position

Incorrect: **Das ist die Frau, die arbeitet in Berlin.**  
Correct: **Das ist die Frau, die in Berlin arbeitet.**

### 3. Forgetting the comma

Incorrect: **Ich kenne jemanden der dir helfen kann.**  
Correct: **Ich kenne jemanden, der dir helfen kann.**

### 4. Choosing the case from the main clause

Do not ask what case the noun has before the comma. Ask what function the relative pronoun has **after the comma**.

## A Simple Three-Step Method

When building a relative clause, use this routine:

1. Find the noun you are describing. This gives you its **gender or plural form**.
2. Look inside the relative clause and decide whether that noun is the **subject, direct object, dative object or part of a prepositional phrase**.
3. Choose the correct relative pronoun and move the conjugated verb to the **end**.

Example:

**Ich spreche mit einem Lehrer. Der Lehrer kommt aus Österreich.**

The noun is masculine (*der Lehrer*). In the second sentence, *der Lehrer* is the subject. So:

**Ich spreche mit einem Lehrer, der aus Österreich kommt.**

Notice that *einem Lehrer* is dative in the main clause, but **der** is nominative in the relative clause. This is exactly why you must determine the two clauses separately.

## Mini Practice

Choose the correct relative pronoun.

1. Das ist der Mann, ___ mir geholfen hat.
2. Kennst du die Frau, ___ wir gestern getroffen haben?
3. Wo ist das Buch, ___ auf dem Tisch lag?
4. Der Student, mit ___ ich gesprochen habe, kommt morgen.
5. Die Leute, ___ wir gedankt haben, waren sehr freundlich.

Answers:

1. **der**
2. **die**
3. **das**
4. **dem**
5. **denen**

## How to Practise Relative Clauses Effectively

Do not begin by memorising long grammar explanations. Take two short sentences and combine them.

**Ich habe einen Bruder. Mein Bruder lebt in Deutschland.**  
→ **Ich habe einen Bruder, der in Deutschland lebt.**

Then change the grammatical role:

**Ich besuche meinen Bruder. Du kennst meinen Bruder.**  
→ **Ich besuche meinen Bruder, den du kennst.**

Practising these transformations trains both **case recognition** and **verb position** at the same time.

## Relative Clauses Help You Move Beyond Basic German

At A1 and A2, learners often communicate through short independent sentences. From B1 onward, relative clauses help you connect information, describe people and objects precisely, and produce more sophisticated speaking and writing.

They are especially useful in descriptions, formal emails, presentations and exam writing:

**Ich suche einen Sprachkurs, der flexible Unterrichtszeiten anbietet.**  
I am looking for a language course that offers flexible lesson times.

**Die Erfahrung, die ich während des Praktikums gesammelt habe, war sehr wertvoll.**  
The experience I gained during the internship was very valuable.

Once the basic pattern is clear, the key is repetition: identify the noun, determine the case inside the relative clause, choose the pronoun, and send the verb to the end.
