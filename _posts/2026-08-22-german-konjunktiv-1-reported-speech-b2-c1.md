## What Is Konjunktiv I in German?

If you read German news, formal reports or academic texts, you will often meet forms such as **sei, habe, werde** and **könne**. These forms belong to **Konjunktiv I**, the mood German frequently uses for **indirect or reported speech (indirekte Rede)**.

Konjunktiv I helps a speaker report what another person said **without presenting the statement as their own fact**.

Direct speech:

> Maria sagt: „Ich bin krank.“  
> Maria says: “I am ill.”

Reported speech:

> Maria sagt, sie **sei** krank.  
> Maria says that she is ill.

The reporter communicates Maria's statement but keeps a useful distance from its truth. This distinction becomes especially valuable at B2 and C1, where learners work with news, arguments, formal writing and summaries.

## The Most Important Konjunktiv I Forms

The verb **sein** is particularly important because its forms are clearly different from the normal indicative:

| Person | Konjunktiv I of *sein* |
|---|---|
| ich | sei |
| du | seiest |
| er/sie/es | sei |
| wir | seien |
| ihr | seiet |
| sie/Sie | seien |

Examples:

> Er sagt, er **sei** müde.  
> He says he is tired.

> Die Mitarbeiter erklären, sie **seien** mit der Entscheidung unzufrieden.  
> The employees explain that they are dissatisfied with the decision.

For many verbs, Konjunktiv I is formed from the infinitive stem plus special endings. The third-person singular is especially useful:

- haben → er **habe**
- kommen → er **komme**
- machen → er **mache**
- werden → er **werde**
- können → er **könne**
- müssen → er **müsse**

> Der Student sagt, er **habe** die Aufgabe bereits gemacht.  
> The student says he has already done the assignment.

> Die Expertin meint, die Situation **werde** sich verbessern.  
> The expert believes the situation will improve.

## Direct Speech to Indirect Speech

A practical way to learn Konjunktiv I is to transform complete statements.

**Direct:**

> Paul sagt: „Ich arbeite heute von zu Hause.“

**Indirect:**

> Paul sagt, er **arbeite** heute von zu Hause.  
> Paul says he is working from home today.

Notice that **ich** changes to **er** because the perspective changes.

Another example:

> Die Ministerin sagt: „Wir brauchen mehr Fachkräfte.“

becomes:

> Die Ministerin sagt, man **brauche** mehr Fachkräfte.

Depending on the context, **sie sagt, sie bräuchten ...** may also appear when the original *wir* refers to a group including the speaker. Pronouns must therefore be adapted to the actual meaning, not changed mechanically.

## What If Konjunktiv I Looks Like the Indicative?

This is one of the most important B2–C1 rules. Some Konjunktiv I forms are identical to ordinary present-tense forms. When that happens, German often uses **Konjunktiv II** so that reported speech remains recognizable.

Indicative:

> Sie sagen: „Wir haben keine Zeit.“

The form *wir haben* would look exactly the same in Konjunktiv I. A clear reported version is therefore:

> Sie sagen, sie **hätten** keine Zeit.  
> They say they have no time.

Similarly:

> Die Teilnehmer sagen, sie **kämen** später.  
> The participants say they will come later.

This does **not** mean that Konjunktiv II always expresses an unreal situation. Here it can simply replace an ambiguous Konjunktiv I form in reported speech.

## Reporting the Past

To report a past event, use the Konjunktiv I form of **haben** or **sein** plus the past participle.

> Er sagt: „Ich habe den Vertrag unterschrieben.“

becomes:

> Er sagt, er **habe den Vertrag unterschrieben**.  
> He says he signed the contract.

With a verb that takes *sein* in the perfect:

> Sie sagt: „Ich bin nach Berlin gefahren.“

becomes:

> Sie sagt, sie **sei nach Berlin gefahren**.  
> She says she travelled to Berlin.

This structure is extremely useful when summarising interviews, reports and source texts.

## Reporting the Future

Use **werde + infinitive**:

> Der Chef sagt: „Die Firma wird neue Mitarbeiter einstellen.“

> Der Chef sagt, die Firma **werde neue Mitarbeiter einstellen**.  
> The boss says the company will hire new employees.

Do not confuse this **werde** with passive voice. Here, *werden + infinitive* reports a future statement.

## Konjunktiv I vs. dass + Indicative

In everyday German, speakers often use **dass** with the indicative:

> Er sagt, dass er krank ist.

This is completely normal. In formal reporting, however, Konjunktiv I can make the distinction between the reporter and the original speaker clearer:

> Er sagt, er **sei** krank.

For advanced learners, the goal is not to replace every *dass* sentence. The goal is to recognize the register and choose the structure that fits the situation.

## Why Konjunktiv I Matters at B2 and C1

At advanced levels, you may need to summarise another person's argument rather than simply state your own opinion. Konjunktiv I gives you an efficient tool for doing this.

Instead of repeatedly writing:

> Der Autor sagt, dass ... Der Autor sagt auch, dass ...

try:

> Der Autor weist darauf hin, die Maßnahme **sei** langfristig notwendig. Außerdem betont er, sie **könne** die Kosten erheblich senken.

This sounds more compact and demonstrates control of formal German.

Useful reporting verbs include:

- **sagen** — to say
- **erklären** — to explain/state
- **behaupten** — to claim
- **betonen** — to emphasize
- **berichten** — to report
- **mitteilen** — to inform/state
- **darauf hinweisen** — to point out
- **argumentieren** — to argue

## Common Mistakes

### 1. Using *würde* for every reported statement

Less precise:

> Er sagt, er **würde** krank sein.

For a simple reported present statement, use:

> Er sagt, er **sei** krank.

### 2. Forgetting to change pronouns

Direct:

> Anna sagt: „Ich habe morgen Zeit.“

Incorrect:

> Anna sagt, ich habe morgen Zeit.

Correct:

> Anna sagt, sie **habe** morgen Zeit.

### 3. Treating reported information as your own claim

Compare:

> Der Politiker sagt, die Reform **ist** erfolgreich.

with:

> Der Politiker sagt, die Reform **sei** erfolgreich.

The second version more clearly signals: *This is the politician's claim, not necessarily mine.*

## A Practical Learning Method

Do not try to memorize every possible Konjunktiv I form at once. Start with the forms that appear constantly in real German: **sei, seien, habe, werde, könne, müsse**.

Then practise a three-step routine:

1. Write one sentence in direct speech.
2. Identify who originally said it and adjust the pronouns.
3. Report it using Konjunktiv I; if the form is identical to the indicative, consider Konjunktiv II.

For example:

> „Wir können das Problem lösen.“  
> Die Experten sagen, sie **könnten** das Problem lösen.

Then add a past and future sentence. Ten minutes of transformation practice is more useful than memorising a long table without context.

## Mini Practice

Change these sentences into reported speech:

1. Lena sagt: „Ich bin sehr zufrieden.“
2. Der Student sagt: „Ich habe die Prüfung bestanden.“
3. Der Geschäftsführer sagt: „Die Firma wird expandieren.“
4. Die Mitarbeiter sagen: „Wir haben zu wenig Zeit.“

Possible answers:

1. Lena sagt, sie **sei** sehr zufrieden.
2. Der Student sagt, er **habe** die Prüfung bestanden.
3. Der Geschäftsführer sagt, die Firma **werde** expandieren.
4. Die Mitarbeiter sagen, sie **hätten** zu wenig Zeit.

## From B2 Understanding to C1 Control

At B2, focus on recognizing Konjunktiv I and confidently using common forms such as **sei, habe** and **werde**. At C1, aim to switch naturally between direct speech, indirect speech, past reporting and appropriate Konjunktiv II replacement forms.

The key question is not simply “Which ending do I need?” Ask instead: **Whose statement am I reporting, and do I want to mark it clearly as reported information?** Once that purpose is clear, Konjunktiv I becomes much easier to use.
